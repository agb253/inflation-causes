What caused US inflation, 2020-2024: a cause-by-cause decomposition with an explicit error term

Technical report. Plain-language version: the web page. Code and data: this folder (run_report.sh rebuilds every number and figure from data/raw/; Python and package versions in requirements.txt).

1. What this can't show (read first)

  • The split between demand and supply is the least settled part. In this model, demand (relief, the Fed, pent-up spending) raises inflation only through the labor market: more demand, fewer unemployed, more job openings per unemployed person, faster wage growth, higher prices. Bernanke and Blanchard, whose wage-price model this is, write that the price shocks of 2021-22 included "sharp increases in commodity prices, reflecting strong aggregate demand, and sectoral price spikes, resulting from changes in the level and sectoral composition of demand together with constraints on sectoral supply" (NBER w31417, abstract). That part of demand is counted here under supply chains and energy. So the demand lines are lower bounds and the supply lines upper bounds. The SF Fed's category-level labels put the size of this at about 1.2 points of the 2021-24 total (range -1.9 to +5.0 depending on how labels are shared out; Section 6), which would make supply and demand about equal.
  • Single lines are imprecise; groups of lines are not. The 90% range for supply chains is +0.8 to +8.3 points and for the COVID drag -12.5 to -0.3. That width is real: it comes from the wage-price model's own coefficients, and three attempts to narrow it with more data failed (Section 5). Sums of lines are much tighter, because their errors offset: all labor-market causes together, +2.2 to +6.3. Read single lines as orders of magnitude and the group totals and the comparisons in Section 5 as the findings.
  • The relief lines depend most on one assumption: how much GDP each relief dollar produced. Scaling the relief effects up to CBO's 2020 total (x2.04) roughly doubles both relief lines; the COVID drag deepens by about the same amount (Section 9). The 90% ranges include this.
  • The COVID drag is estimated on the same 2020-22 data as everything else (no outside estimate exists). It is fitted to the unemployment that relief, the Fed and pent-up demand leave unexplained, so it partly mirrors their size.
  • The Fed is identified with a borderline instrument (first-stage F = 9.5; robust 8.2; the usual threshold is 10). The cleaner version (MPS_ORTH, which the SF Fed calls a better measure of new policy information because it removes predictable variation) is a weak instrument (F = 3.6) and gives Fed effects several times larger.
  • Energy & food are assigned to causes by date, not estimated: 2020Q1-2021Q4 to COVID, 2022Q1-2023Q1 to the war in Ukraine (which therefore also contains the October 2022 OPEC+ cut and the late-2022 bird-flu egg spike), after 2023Q1 to "other & not explained".
  • One model family. A model with a steeper Phillips curve, or one where Fed credibility or the dollar matter directly, would give demand and the Fed larger roles.

2. Main results

These are the model's own estimates. Section 8 updates them with independent evidence the model doesn't use (wage data, differences across spending categories, other countries); that is the best estimate.

PCE inflation added up to 17.3 points over 2021-2024 (the four Q4-over-Q4 rates). The model explains 16.2 of them. The economy's pre-2020 momentum accounts for 8.2 (about 2% a year); the other 9.0 points are what needs explaining.

Findings the ranges support:

  1. Supply shocks were probably the largest new force, but the model doesn't settle it. Supply chains, the war, the worker shortage and the 2021 energy rebound add up to 4.7 of the 6.9 points in 2022Q2 (an upper bound; see Section 1). Over 2021-24, supply chains + war + worker shortage exceed relief + pent-up + Fed in 67% of redraws; without the worker shortage, in 39% (Section 5).
  2. Relief laws mattered, under both presidents, and probably neither was the largest single cause (supply chains exceed Biden-signed relief in 72% of redraws, but all relief together in only 41%). Biden-signed relief: +2.1 [+1.0 to +5.6] points over 2021-24; Trump-signed: +1.7 [+0.7 to +5.1]. Together +3.7 [+1.8 to +10.8], against 9.0 points of inflation beyond the pre-2020 pace. Much of relief's effect offset the COVID drag (-4.3): the shutdown pushed unemployment up and inflation down, and relief pushed the other way.
  3. The Fed's net effect over 2021-24 was small: +0.4 [-0.1 to +1.5] points. Its 2022-23 hikes were still working their way through at the end of 2024.
  4. Greedflation is about zero in the model (-0.0 [-0.2 to +0.1]), although profit margins did rise in 2021 by accounting (Table 3 of TABLE_UNIFIED.md). Margin moves beyond what costs, shortages and demand explain don't predict PCE inflation in 1990-2024.
  5. Pent-up demand added 1.2 points [0.5 to 2.1].

3. Did the Fed act too late?

Tested directly: the model is re-run with earlier hikes and compared with actual policy (src/fed_late_report.py, fig15_fed_late.png).

The actual 2022-23 hikes, a year earlier+0.01-0.40 [-1.08, +0.09]+0.29+1.34 [-0.00, +2.81]

The published estimate for the first path (Barnichon, FRBSF Economic Letter 2022-28, Oct 11 2022): "inflation would have been about 1 percentage point lower as of June 2022, while unemployment would be about 2 percentage points higher" (95%: 0.2-2 lower; 1-3 higher). Its measure is core inflation; it uses rate effects estimated on 1990-2007.

What this supports: starting a year earlier would have lowered inflation, by 0.2 to 1 point depending on the model, and every estimate puts the price at roughly 2 to 3.5 points of extra unemployment for each point of inflation removed. Starting earlier would not have prevented most of the surge. What it can't settle: whether the Fed's framework at the time (flexible average inflation targeting) and its forward guidance raised inflation expectations in ways a model like this one, where expectations follow past inflation, doesn't capture.

4. Method

One chain, every link estimated on public data or taken from a cited official source. Equations line by line, every quarter, and every data source with links: TABLE_UNIFIED.md. Long-form notes: METHOD_UNIFIED.md.

  1. Relief laws → GDP. BEA payments by program (23 programs) x CBO's GDP-per-dollar multipliers (CBO 56537, Table 3), spread over time with geometric weights fitted to CBO's timing (lambda = 0.74). Payments before March 2021 are Trump's; from March 2021, Biden's (the rule least favourable to "Trump's relief mattered too").
  2. Pent-up demand → GDP. Real PCE (BEA DPCERX) below its 2015-2019 log-linear trend in 2020-21, summed: forced saving of $786 billion (2017 dollars). Spent evenly from 2021Q2 through 2023Q3 (the SF Fed's date for excess savings running out), x 0.78 GDP per dollar (CBO "other spending"). Relief money is excluded, since the relief lines already count its spending. The method follows Aladangady, Cho, Feiveson & Pinto (FEDS Notes, Oct 21 2022), whose Table 1 shows transfers, not reduced spending, were the main source of excess savings.
  3. GDP → unemployment: Okun's law, beta = -0.815, estimated 1990-2019.
  4. Fed → unemployment. Monthly proxy SVAR (Bauer-Swanson data, 1977-2019), instrument = monetary-policy surprise MPS, F = 9.5. Counterfactual rate paths via Sims-Zha policy shocks inside each episode window.
  5. COVID shutdown → unemployment, estimated: u-change not explained above = g0 s_t + g1 s_t-1 + e_t, OLS 2020Q1-2022Q4, no constant; s = Oxford stringency index / 100. g0 = 15.25 (s.e. 2.33), g1 = -4.28 (s.e. 2.36), R^2 = 0.80 (uncentered, since there is no constant: 0.95). After the tracker ends (2022) the index fades to 0 by 2023Q2, when the federal Public Health Emergency ended (HHS). The residual e_t is not assigned to any cause; it is part of "other & not explained".
  6. Unemployment → V/U: log(V/U) on u, 1990-2019. V/U beyond what unemployment implies = worker shortage.
  7. V/U, energy, food, supply chains (NY Fed GSCPI), margins → PCE inflation: Bernanke-Blanchard wage-price model, re-estimated on PCE.
  8. Attribution: each cause = model inflation with every cause minus model inflation without that cause. The model is linear, so the causes add up to the model's estimate; the gap to actual inflation is reported, not assigned.

What changed from the previous version, and why. (a) The COVID line was a leftover forced to match unemployment; it is now estimated, and what it misses is shown as error. (b) The Fed instrument was MPS_ORTH (F = 3.6, weak), which implied the 2022-23 hikes raised 2024 unemployment by about 4 points; it is now MPS (F = 9.5). (c) Pent-up demand is estimated instead of sitting inside the leftover. (d) The "did the Fed act too late?" test is new.

5. Uncertainty: why some ranges are wide, and what the data do pin down

Each of 1,000 redraws varies: the wage-price coefficients; Okun beta; the V/U coefficients; one wild-bootstrap replication of the Fed VAR and instrument; relief timing (CBO-fitted or Hutchins) x coverage (1 or 2.04); the Sims-Zha ridge; the COVID coefficients; pent-up demand's multiplier (0.78 or 1.0) and spending period (to 2023Q3 or 2024Q4). Half the redraws use relief coverage 2.04, so the relief and COVID ranges are not centred on the point estimate (Biden and Trump relief combined: median +4.8 against point +3.7). Ranges are reported at 68% (16th-84th percentile) and 90% (5th-95th); every cause, every quarter: output/reproduce_long.csv.

Three attempts to narrow the single lines, none adopted (code in src/uncertainty/, logs in output/):

  1. Weight the coefficient redraws by how well they reproduce observed 2020-24 wage growth (data the wage equation never saw). The COVID range narrows only modestly, the supply-chain range not at all, and too few redraws carry the weight (ESS 47 of 600) for the result to be stable. Log: uncertainty_condition.log.
  2. Re-estimate the wage and expectations equations through 2024 instead of 2019. The ranges barely change. Log: uncertainty_extend_sample.log.
  3. Let the unemployment data choose the relief multiplier. An earlier version of this report said the lockdown fit was equally good at every relief size (centred R^2 0.77 to 0.80). That comparison was wrong: each relief size leaves a different series for the lockdown term to explain, so their R^2 values can't be ranked. Compared properly, on unemployment the model misses (E15 D), the fit gets steadily better as relief gets smaller, all the way down to a quarter of the CBO-based effect. That happens because 12 quarters can't separate relief from the lockdown term: the lockdown term absorbs whatever relief doesn't explain. It also conflicts with the cross-country evidence (Section 6), which puts relief near the model's own size. Not adopted. Logs: uncertainty_relief_scale.log, ext/e15_wages_ui_ranges.log.

Narrowing these lines needs information this model doesn't use: category-level price and quantity data (the Shapiro method), or cross-country variation in relief size (the Jorda et al. method). Both are tried in Section 6.

SF Fed supply/demand labels (Shapiro; output/shapiro_leakage.csv). Shapiro labels each spending category's price change as supply-driven (price and quantity move opposite ways), demand-driven (same way) or ambiguous. That is a different question from "which cause", so the two can't be weighted against each other directly. Three of the model's lines have no counterpart in his labels: the lingering COVID drag on jobs, the pre-existing momentum above its 2019 level, and "not explained"; his method spreads them over all labels. With c = the share of those lines his method labels supply, and a = the share of "ambiguous" that is really supply:

leakage L = model supply lines + c x (no-counterpart lines) - (Shapiro supply + a x ambiguous)

L is the part of the model's supply lines that Shapiro's data label as demand: the "demand meeting limited supply" of Section 1. Setting a and c to his supply share of labelled inflation:

COVID drag and wages

  • E1. Kinked Phillips curve (Benigno-Eggertsson): with a threshold at V/U = 1, the extra slope above it is -0.451 (0.842) in the wage equation and +0.913 (0.800) in the price equation; no threshold from 0.6 to 1.4 is significant. Not adopted.
  • E13. Did wages slow in 2020? The model's wage equation predicts 4-quarter wage growth of 1.4% by 2020Q4; actual ECI growth was 2.8%, and the Atlanta Fed Wage Growth Tracker (same workers, so no layoff distortion) 3.7%, unchanged from 2019. The COVID drag works mainly through that predicted slowdown, so it is overstated.
  • E5. States. Lockdowns: with quarter effects (which remove everything national), each unit of stringency raised a state's unemployment by 4.7 (1.6) points at once and 4.3 (1.3) a quarter later: about the same total as the national fit, which supports it. Wage curve: state wage growth falls 0.35 per point of unemployment below 6% and less above it (change +0.15, s.e. 0.20), flatter at high unemployment, as the COVID evidence suggests, but not significantly.

Relief

  • E7. Cross-country, quarterly (core CPI, 2021Q4): US relief effect +1.9 (1.0) against the model's 1.2 for the same quarter; 2021Q4 + 2022Q4 is uninformative (+0.7, s.e. 2.5).
  • E6. What recipients spent (Opportunity Insights card data). Third stimulus checks: card spending ran 1.5% above trend in the six weeks after March 15, 2021 (8.3% for the lowest-income quarter); scaled to all spending, about 7% of the $390 billion within six weeks (rough: card data miss rent, utilities and health). States that ended the $300 unemployment supplement early (25 states, June-July 2021): low-income card spending fell 1.0% (s.e. 0.5) relative to other states. Too coarse to replace CBO's multipliers; nothing here suggests they are too low.

  • E9. Credibility. On the day of the new framework (Aug 27, 2020) the 5-year, 5-year forward breakeven rose +0.05 points over two days, inside the range of ordinary 2015-19 moves; it went from 1.81% the day before to 2.25% at end-2021 (2015-19 range 1.41-2.35%; 2021-22 peak 2.67%). Market measures show long-run expectations drifting up toward the top of their pre-COVID range, not a break. Breakevens also contain risk and liquidity premia.

Greed

  • E10. Margins by industry (BEA input-output accounts): economy-wide, gross operating surplus rose +1.1 points of output in 2021, +0.4 in 2022 and +1.4 in 2023 from its 2015-19 average. The largest rises were in water transportation, mining, farms, wood and chemical products, where supply was tight: scarcity rents, which Bernanke and Blanchard (2024, footnote 6) also suspect, rather than broad-based markups. (Restaurants and hotels rise partly because PPP loans count as subsidies.)

Wages, unemployment checks, and why the ranges are wide (src/extensions/e15_wages_ui_ranges.py)

  • E15 A. Were wages held down before COVID, and did 2021-24 correct it? Labor's share of nonfarm business income averaged 109 (index, 2017 = 100) in the 1990s and 99.5 in the 2010s (BLS via FRED PRS85006173): pay did fall behind productivity. But 2020-24 did not reverse it:

Real pay rose about half as fast as productivity, so labor's share fell further. A catch-up term in the wage equation (last quarter's labor-share gap, 1990-2019) is small and not significant: -0.026 (s.e. 0.019) on the gap from the 1990-2019 average, +0.066 (0.058) on the gap from trend; either way it adds at most 0.4 points to any quarter's wage growth. Where catch-up did happen is at the bottom: the lowest-paid quarter of workers gained the most after inflation (Atlanta Fed Wage Growth Tracker, same workers a year apart; cumulative from December 2019 to December 2024, approximate):

So COVID gave low-wage workers leverage, but across all workers prices rose faster than pay, not the reverse. Wage catch-up is not a separate cause of the inflation; the low-wage gains are part of the worker-shortage and V/U lines.

Their other possible effect, keeping people out of work, would show up in the worker-shortage line. Test: 25 states ended the $300 supplement early (June 12 to July 31, 2021), the rest in September:

Not done

  • Pooling the supply-chain effect across countries: Bernanke & Blanchard's 11-economy study uses a different shortage measure in each country (Google searches, the GSCPI, surveys) and posts no data, so coefficients can't be pooled.
  • Splitting relief into household transfers vs loans across countries: the IMF table gives only health vs non-health.
  • Formal model averaging by forecast record: 12 forecast quarters are too few for stable weights; E11 is shown instead.
  • A full joint estimation of every link: replaced by sequential updating (Section 8), which uses the same evidence but keeps each link's own estimate as the starting point.

What the evidence changes. Read with the out-of-sample and state results above:

  1. Supply chains: +5.4 [+2.5 to +9.2]. The category panel finds a larger direct effect than the model's own, which raises the bottom of the range (from about 1 to 2.5). But the out-of-sample test (E11) and the other supply measures (E4) point the other way: most of this line is knock-on through wages and expectations, and the model's knock-on looks too persistent. Supply chains were clearly a major cause; the size is still uncertain by a factor of about three.
  2. COVID drag: -5.6 [-10.7 to -1.8]. Narrower by about a third. The cross-country evidence removes the deepest drags (which come with the largest relief effects). The wage data remove the shallowest: a model in which wages follow V/U in a straight line needs a strong V/U effect to explain the 2021-22 wage surge, and that same strength implies a deep 2020 drag. E13 shows wages did not in fact slow in 2020, so the true drag may be smaller than any version of this linear model can produce; the state wage curve (E5) hints that wages respond less when unemployment is high, though not significantly.
  3. Relief paid under Biden: +2.4 [+1.2 to +4.6]; paid under Trump: +2.3 [+1.1 to +4.5]. The cross-country evidence trims the high-multiplier cases and the wage data the smallest; each president's relief is now about 1 to 4.6 points, median about 2.4.
  4. Supply versus demand: with the category panel, supply chains + war and relief + pent-up demand + the Fed come out even (51% of weighted redraws put supply first); counting the worker shortage as supply, 78%. The SF Fed labels (Section 6) also say about even. The data support "both, in similar measure", not "mostly supply" or "mostly demand".
  5. The Fed: the evidence above doesn't touch the Fed lines; E8 says they are more likely too small than too large.
